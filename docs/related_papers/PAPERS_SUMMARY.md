# Related Papers Summary

## Cenci & Saavedra (2018) — *Structural Stability of Nonlinear Population Dynamics* (Phys. Rev. E)
Extends the structural stability framework — previously limited to linear Lotka-Volterra dynamics — to a broad class of nonlinear functional responses. Also extends it to stochastic systems with finite populations. Core takeaway: structural stability analysis doesn't require linearity.

## Cenci et al. (2018) — *Estimating the Effect of the Reorganization of Interactions on the Adaptability of Species to Changing Environments* (J. Theoretical Biology)
Introduces the concept of an **adaptation space** — the set of environmental conditions that a group of species can make compatible with its persistence through reorganization of interactions. Groups with larger adaptation spaces are more likely to persist under novel environments. Shows that species present in both control and perturbed communities tend to have the largest adaptation spaces, providing a framework for identifying conservation priorities.

## Cenci et al. (2018) — *Rethinking the Importance of the Structure of Ecological Networks Under an Environment-Dependent Framework* (Ecology & Evolution)
Argues that the importance of any network structure (e.g., nestedness, modularity) cannot be assessed independently of the environmental context — conclusions about structural advantage are inconsistent when drawn from perturbation-tolerance alone. Proposes an **environment-dependent framework** that links network structure to community dynamics under varying external conditions, revealing the limits at which communities can tolerate environmental change.

## Saavedra (2024) — *The Feasibility Principle in Community Ecology*
Proposes a unifying "feasibility principle" with 3 postulates: communities persist for environmental conditions constrained by their interaction structure; self-organization maximizes that feasibility domain; and transitions follow least-change paths in environmental space. Frames conservation in terms of feasibility under climate change.

## Song (2024) — *Assembly Graph as the Rosetta Stone of Ecological Assembly*
Introduces the **assembly graph** (nodes = species combinations, edges = transitions via invasion) as a common visual language uniting dynamical, informational, and probabilistic theories of ecological assembly. A math-light review designed to catalyze empirical research.

## Song & Levine (2025) — *Rigorous (In)validation / Validation of Ecological Models* (bioRxiv preprint + Nature Ecology & Evolution)
These are the preprint and published versions of the same paper. Introduces **covariance criteria** (borrowed from queueing theory) as a rigorous, non-parametric test for model validity using covariance structure of time-series data. Applied to predator-prey functional responses, eco-evolutionary dynamics, and higher-order interactions.

## Song et al. (2025) — *A Geometric Approach to Beta Diversity* (Ecological Monographs)
Reframes beta diversity as the **hypervolume** of a geometric embedding of a metacommunity. Accounts for species associations and non-additivity (unlike classic metrics). Resolves long-standing debates about latitudinal gradients and sampling effect effects.

## Song et al. (2017) — *Why Are Some Plant–Pollinator Networks More Nested Than Others?* (J. Animal Ecology)
Analyzes 59 plant-pollinator networks and finds that **higher nestedness correlates with higher temperature seasonality**. Argues this is because nested structures increase the range of environmental conditions compatible with coexistence (structural stability), which is more advantageous in variable environments.

## Song et al. (2023) — *Rapid Monitoring of Ecological Persistence* (PNAS)
Shows that persistence of small **subnetworks (motifs)** reliably predicts persistence of the whole network. Provides a practical biomonitoring tool: you can assess ecosystem health from incomplete sampling, and it's easier to detect non-persistence than persistence.

## Song & Saavedra (2018) — *Structural Stability as a Consistent Predictor of Phenological Events* (Proc. R. Soc. B)
Derives a structural stability measure from plant-pollinator interaction networks and tests it as a predictor of daily species richness changes in a high-arctic community. Finds it's the **only consistent predictor** across ecological and environmental variables — outperforming temperature, humidity, etc.
